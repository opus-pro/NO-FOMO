03Oct 27

Brooks’s Law meets multi-agent software development

Jan van den Berg revisits Brooks's Law in the context of AI coding agents. His argument is that sequential dependencies survive automation, while coordination overhead shifts toward defining completion, reviewing code, catching hallucinations, and integrating changes. Adding agents without clear requirements can therefore accelerate the production of incorrect work. This is an engineering opinion essay, but it gives teams a useful framing for separating raw implementation capacity from successful project delivery.

02Oct 25

Thinking-in-Bets reduces mistaken reversals after feedback

Thinking-in-Bets asks an agent to record its claims, supporting checks, and confidence before reading user feedback. The authors find that this training-free step helps agents preserve correct work while investigating genuine mistakes. Across coding, reports, reasoning, finance, and terminal tasks, the timing of the record matters: writing it after criticism recovers little of the benefit. The approach offers a concrete way to study sycophancy without simply telling agents to resist all corrections.

Skill-infinity optimizes the skill optimizer itself

Skill-infinity makes an agent's skill-optimization procedure itself a reusable, editable skill. A meta agent diagnoses traces, proposes candidates, requests reviews, and allocates search effort while also improving that procedure. On 83 SkillsBench tasks, the authors report a 60.7 percent pass rate, compared with 56.4 percent for human-curated skills and 54.5 percent for SkillEvolver. The result is evidence within this benchmark, rather than a demonstration of unrestricted or universal recursive self-improvement.

Video memory can preserve the past but break the present

VMem-Bench studies whether retrieving previous visual observations reliably improves long-video generation. Across controlled tests, retrieval increases entity recall but reduces precision and avoidance of forbidden entities; it can also preserve outdated appearances after a story changes them. The paper separates historical visual fidelity from correctness in the current narrative. That distinction matters for generative-video systems: more memory is not automatically useful if retrieval ignores which characters or appearances should now be absent.

S3AC revisits actor-critic training for LLM reasoning

S3AC introduces a critic-training recipe for long-chain reasoning, providing token-specific advantages and prompt-specific baselines. Its Difficulty-Aware Prompt Sampling uses estimated difficulty and outcome history to select prompts before generation, reducing wasted rollouts. The authors report improvements across dense and mixture-of-experts models from 1.5 billion to 397 billion parameters, including sustained single-rollout training at 35 billion. The work suggests that carefully stabilized critics can address limitations of popular critic-free reinforcement-learning approaches.

Delta-tau benchmark tests agents as tools change

Delta-tau Bench evaluates agents in tool ecosystems where services disappear, change, or acquire more efficient alternatives. It contains 1,588 validated multi-turn tasks across 42 domains and six ecosystem states. The strongest evaluated model or harness reaches only 42.3 percent success, according to the authors. Their Steward manager maintains tools and procedural knowledge together. The benchmark emphasizes that remembering yesterday's successful workflow is insufficient when the available capabilities themselves are changing.

Continual Learning Exam measures learning across work

Continual Learning Exam organizes 120 expert-authored professional tasks into five-level ladders, producing 600 levels across 89 roles and 12 occupational domains. Later stages require using earlier experience. Across 17 model-harness configurations, feedback and revision bring a median gain of 5.3 percentage points, yet the best system completes all five levels on only 23.3 percent of tasks. The benchmark challenges claims of sustained learning by measuring progress across connected work rather than isolated answers.

08Oct 23

FORK rewards precision alongside rubric coverage

The FORK reward addresses a problem in rubric-based reinforcement learning: maximizing coverage can encourage long responses containing unnecessary or incorrect material. It combines recall with a precision objective that checks whether each response unit is needed and factually correct. The authors report shorter answers and better preference results at similar rubric coverage. The study makes reward design directly relevant to product quality, showing why checking that required points appear may leave harmful incentives unaddressed.
